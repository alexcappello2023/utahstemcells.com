Regenerative medicine has grown fast, and so has the number of clinics marketing "stem cell therapy" for everything from joint pain to neurological disease. Not all of them are equally careful, transparent, or safe. If you are researching options for [knee osteoarthritis](/knee-osteoarthritis/) or chronic [joint pain](/joint-pain/), knowing how to tell a well-run, physician-led practice from a marketing-first operation can protect your health, your time, and your peace of mind.

This guide walks through the specific red flags regulators, patient advocacy groups, and regenerative medicine physicians consistently point to, along with the questions you should feel comfortable asking any clinic before you move forward.

## Why Vetting a Clinic Matters

Regenerative medicine, including [stem cell joint regeneration](/stem-cell-joint-regeneration/) and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy, is a genuinely promising and evolving field. But "promising" is not the same as "proven for every condition," and the gap between the two is exactly where less scrupulous marketing tends to live. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has repeatedly issued public warnings about clinics offering unapproved stem cell products, sometimes tied to serious adverse events such as infections or vision loss. That does not mean regenerative treatments are unsafe in general — it means the source, processing, and physician oversight behind any specific treatment matter enormously.

## Common Red Flags to Watch For

### 1. Promises of a "cure" or guaranteed results

No legitimate regenerative medicine provider can honestly promise a cure or a specific outcome. Biology varies from patient to patient, and current evidence for most stem cell and PRP applications shows a range of possible improvement, not guaranteed reversal of disease. If a clinic tells you with certainty that you will regrow cartilage, reverse arthritis, or eliminate a chronic condition entirely, treat that as a warning sign rather than reassurance.

### 2. Vague or evasive answers about cell sourcing

You should be able to ask a straightforward question — "Where do these cells come from, and how are they processed?" — and get a clear, specific answer. A trustworthy clinic will explain plainly whether a product is donor-derived umbilical or amniotic tissue prepared under laboratory standards, or whether a therapy like PRP uses your own blood (autologous). Clinics that dodge the question, call their process "proprietary and secret," or can't name the tissue bank or lab they work with deserve extra scrutiny.

### 3. One product marketed for unrelated conditions

Be cautious if the exact same injection or infusion is marketed as a fix for joint pain, hair loss, neurological disease, and anti-aging all at once. Biologically, it is unlikely that one undifferentiated product performs equally well across such different tissues and conditions. Reputable practices tailor recommendations — for example, treating [hip pain](/hip-pain/) differently than [shoulder pain](/shoulder-pain/) — based on your specific evaluation rather than a one-size-fits-all pitch.

### 4. No named, licensed treating physician

Ask who will actually evaluate you and perform or supervise the treatment, and confirm their license and training. If a clinic is reluctant to name the treating physician, share credentials, or explain their experience with your specific condition, that is a meaningful gap in accountability. Physician-led care means a licensed doctor is directly involved in your diagnosis, treatment plan, and follow-up — not just a technician following a script.

### 5. Dismissing FDA oversight entirely

Some clinics tell patients "the FDA doesn't regulate this" as a way of sidestepping hard questions. In reality, FDA regulation depends on how a cell product is processed and used; minimally manipulated products used for their normal function fall under different rules than more heavily processed ones. A trustworthy provider can explain, in plain language, how their specific product and process relate to FDA rules — not wave the topic away.

### 6. Evidence based only on testimonials

Before-and-after photos and patient stories can be compelling, but they are not clinical evidence. Ask whether the clinic can point you to published research, even preliminary studies, that relate to your condition and the specific treatment being offered. A responsible provider will be upfront about where the evidence is strong, where it is still emerging, and where it simply doesn't exist yet.

### 7. Pressure to decide quickly

"Limited-time pricing," countdown-style urgency, or discouraging you from getting a second opinion are sales tactics, not medical ones. A good consultation should feel like an evaluation of your health, not a closing pitch. You should always feel free to take your paperwork home, think it over, and ask a second physician for their opinion.

## Autologous vs. Donor-Derived: Know the Difference

Part of vetting a clinic is understanding what is actually being injected. At Utah Stem Cells, regenerative options generally fall into two categories:

- **Donor-derived cellular tissue** (from screened umbilical cord or amniotic sources, processed in a lab) — used in options like [stem cell joint regeneration](/stem-cell-joint-regeneration/) and [IV stem cell therapy](/iv-stem-cell-therapy/).
- **Autologous PRP**, drawn from your own blood and concentrated for platelet-rich plasma injections, as used in treatments like the [O-Shot](/o-shot/) and [P-Shot](/p-shot/).

Ask any clinic explicitly which category their product falls into, since these have different regulatory pathways and different safety and evidence profiles. A clinic that can't answer this clearly is a clinic worth walking away from.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is stem cell therapy FDA-approved?

Most stem cell and regenerative products used in orthopedic and wellness settings are not "FDA-approved" in the way a drug is approved; regulation depends on how the product is sourced and processed. Ask any clinic to explain, specifically, how their product is regulated rather than accepting a blanket "yes" or "no."

### What's the difference between autologous and donor-derived cells?

Autologous means the material comes from your own body, as with PRP drawn from your blood. Donor-derived means the tissue comes from a screened donor source, such as umbilical cord or amniotic tissue processed in a lab. Both have different safety profiles, evidence bases, and regulatory considerations, and a transparent clinic will explain which one applies to your treatment.

### Can stem cell therapy cure arthritis or chronic pain?

No responsible provider can promise a cure. Current evidence suggests some patients experience meaningful symptom improvement, but outcomes vary and are not guaranteed. Be skeptical of any clinic that promises complete reversal of a chronic condition.

### How do I verify a clinic's credentials?

Ask for the treating physician's name, license, and specific experience with your condition. You can also independently verify medical licensure through your state's medical board and ask the clinic directly which regulatory pathway applies to the specific product they use.

### Should I get a second opinion before treatment?

Yes. A trustworthy clinic will never discourage you from seeking a second opinion. If a provider pressures you to decide immediately or discourages outside consultation, treat that as a significant warning sign.

### What questions should I bring to my first consultation?

Ask where the cells or tissue come from, what evidence supports the treatment for your specific condition, who will perform the procedure, what follow-up care is included, and what realistic range of outcomes you might expect.
