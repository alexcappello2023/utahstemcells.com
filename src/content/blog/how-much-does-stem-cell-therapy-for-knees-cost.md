If you've been living with knee pain and started researching regenerative options, one question comes up almost immediately: what does this actually cost? It's a fair question, and the honest answer is that it depends — on the protocol, the clinic, and how much joint damage needs to be addressed. This guide breaks down realistic price ranges, the factors that move the number up or down, and how stem cell therapy compares financially to more invasive alternatives like [knee replacement](/knee-osteoarthritis/).

## Typical price ranges for knee stem cell therapy

Across the industry, stem cell therapy for knees generally falls somewhere between $3,000 and $15,000 per knee, with most patients landing in the $5,000-$8,000 range for a single comprehensive session. A few factors shape where a given case falls in that range:

- **Simple, localized injections** for mild to moderate joint pain tend to sit at the lower end, roughly $3,000-$5,000.
- **More involved protocols** — larger doses, multiple injection sites, or combination approaches — can run $5,000-$15,000.
- **Bilateral treatment** (both knees) costs more than a single knee, though many clinics offer a reduced rate for the second joint.

These figures are broadly consistent across the regenerative medicine field, though every clinic sets its own pricing based on the specific product used, the physician's expertise, and the level of imaging guidance involved.

## What actually drives the price

A quote isn't just a number pulled out of the air — several concrete variables affect it:

**How advanced the joint damage is.** Early-stage [osteoarthritis](/osteoarthritis/) may respond to a more conservative protocol, while more advanced cases sometimes call for a stronger regenerative approach, additional sessions, or a combination of [PRP joint treatment](/prp-joint-treatment/) with cellular therapy.

**What's actually being injected.** This matters more than most patients realize, and it's where sourcing gets confused across the industry. At Utah Stem Cells, the cellular material used comes from donated umbilical cord and amniotic tissue — screened, lab-processed allogeneic tissue, not cells harvested from your own bone marrow or fat. This is different from clinics that draw and re-inject a patient's own bone marrow concentrate, which is a separate (and typically more invasive, and often costlier) procedure. PRP, by contrast, genuinely is autologous — it's your own blood, concentrated and re-injected — and is frequently used alongside cellular therapy as a complementary step.

**Imaging and diagnostics.** Ultrasound or fluoroscopic guidance during injection adds precision and, often, cost. Pre-treatment MRI or X-ray imaging to confirm the joint's condition is usually billed separately.

**Number of joints and sessions.** One knee versus two, and a single visit versus a planned series, both scale the total investment.

**Follow-up care.** Physical therapy, activity modification guidance, and any recommended booster sessions are part of the full picture, not just the injection itself.

## Does insurance cover it?

Generally, no — most insurance plans, including Medicare, classify stem cell injections for orthopedic use as investigational and do not reimburse the procedure itself. That said, related costs are often covered or partially covered:

- Consultations, X-rays, and MRIs are frequently billed through insurance as standard diagnostic visits.
- Many patients use FSA or HSA funds to offset the out-of-pocket cost.
- Some clinics offer financing plans that spread payments over several months.

Before scheduling anything, it's worth asking your clinic directly what's included in the quoted price versus billed separately — imaging, follow-up visits, and any additional injections should be spelled out up front.

## How the cost compares to other options

It helps to see stem cell therapy for knees next to the alternatives patients are usually weighing it against.

**Versus knee replacement surgery.** Total knee replacement typically runs $30,000-$70,000 when you factor in the surgery itself, anesthesia, hospital stay, and an extended physical therapy course. Beyond the sticker price, replacement surgery usually means weeks of restricted mobility and one to three months away from normal activity. Regenerative approaches are outpatient, with a much shorter recovery window — though it's important to be clear-eyed that they aren't a guaranteed substitute for surgery in every case, particularly with end-stage joint damage. Our article on whether you can [avoid knee replacement with stem cells](/stem-cells-for-knee-pain-can-you-avoid-knee-replacement/) goes deeper into who is and isn't a good candidate for that comparison.

**Versus PRP alone.** [PRP joint treatment](/prp-joint-treatment/) is generally less expensive than cellular therapy, often in the $500-$1,500 per session range, but it works differently — using your own platelet-rich plasma to support the joint's healing response rather than introducing donor-derived cellular material. Many patients start with PRP and add cellular therapy later, or the two are combined in a single visit. We compare the two approaches directly in [PRP vs. stem cells for joint pain](/prp-vs-stem-cells-which-treatment-is-right-for-your-joint-pain/).

**Versus doing nothing.** Untreated joint pain often means ongoing costs too — pain medication, repeated physical therapy, missed work, and a joint that may continue to decline. That's not a reason to rush into any particular treatment, but it's part of a realistic cost comparison.

## Who is a candidate for this investment

Cost only matters in the context of whether the treatment is likely to help you specifically. Good candidates for a joint-focused regenerative protocol typically have:

- Diagnosed osteoarthritis or joint degeneration confirmed by imaging
- Pain or stiffness that limits daily activity but hasn't progressed to bone-on-bone collapse
- A preference to explore non-surgical options before considering replacement surgery
- Realistic expectations about outcomes — this is not an FDA-approved cure, and results vary from person to person

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the average cost of stem cell therapy for one knee?
Most patients pay between $3,000 and $15,000 for a single knee, with a large share landing in the $5,000-$8,000 range. The exact figure depends on the protocol, imaging needs, and whether it's combined with PRP.

### Is stem cell therapy for knees covered by insurance?
Typically not for the injection itself, since most insurers consider it investigational. Diagnostic visits, imaging, and consultations are often billed separately and may be covered.

### Is it cheaper than knee replacement surgery?
Usually yes, especially once you factor in the surgery cost, hospital stay, and extended recovery time associated with replacement. However, it isn't a direct substitute in every case, and a physician needs to evaluate whether it's appropriate for your joint's condition.

### Does Utah Stem Cells use my own stem cells?
No. The cellular material used is derived from donated umbilical cord and amniotic tissue, processed under lab conditions — not harvested from your own bone marrow or fat. PRP, when used alongside it, does come from your own blood.

### How many sessions will I need?
This varies by individual. Some patients see meaningful improvement from a single session; others benefit from a planned series or periodic booster treatments. Your care team will outline this during your evaluation.

### Are results guaranteed?
No. Stem cell therapy for joints is not an FDA-approved cure for osteoarthritis, and outcomes vary from patient to patient based on the degree of joint damage and individual response. A physician evaluation is the only way to set realistic expectations for your case.
