Search "PRP and stem cell therapy" and you'll find the two terms tangled together almost everywhere — compared, combined, sometimes used as if they're interchangeable. They're not. PRP and stem cell therapy are built from different biological material, they're sourced differently, and they tend to be a better fit for different problems. If you're trying to figure out which one is actually relevant to your [joint pain](/joint-pain/), the honest answer starts with understanding what each one is actually for — not just how they compare on paper.

## What PRP is actually for

PRP — platelet-rich plasma — is made from **your own blood**. A small sample is drawn and spun in a centrifuge to concentrate the platelets and the growth factors they carry, then injected into the joint that's bothering you. Nothing about it is donated or outside material: it's autologous by definition, your own blood, concentrated and returned to you.

What PRP is actually good for is delivering a dose of your body's own healing signals right where a joint is irritated or mildly worn — to help calm inflammation and support the repair process that's already underway, just not keeping up. [PRP joint treatment](/prp-joint-treatment/) tends to be the first regenerative option physicians reach for with earlier-stage wear, tendon irritation, or joints that are achy but not yet badly degraded.

## What stem cell therapy is actually for

This is where the sourcing question matters most, and where a lot of online information gets it wrong. At Utah Stem Cells, the stem cells used are **not** taken from your own fat or bone marrow. They come from **donated umbilical cord and amniotic tissue** — collected from healthy, consented donors, processed in a lab, and screened before use. Nothing is harvested from your body to make them. That's the opposite of PRP, and it's worth reading more about if you want the full picture of [why donated tissue is used instead of your own](/umbilical-cord-stem-cells-why-donated-tissue-and-not-your-own/).

Stem cell therapy is actually for joints and injuries where more support is needed than growth factors alone can offer — more advanced [knee osteoarthritis](/knee-osteoarthritis/), joints that haven't responded to PRP or other conservative care, or cases where a physician judges that the added cellular and signaling components are a better match for how much the joint has degraded. In practice, [stem cell joint regeneration](/stem-cell-joint-regeneration/) at Utah Stem Cells typically combines your own PRP with donor-derived stem cells in a single procedure, rather than using stem cells alone.

## How they actually work, side by side

- **PRP** concentrates platelets and growth factors from your blood. Once injected, those factors are thought to signal nearby tissue to ramp up its own repair activity — it's a boost to a process your body already runs.
- **Donor-derived stem cells** bring additional regenerative and signaling components that aren't present in your blood sample alone. The intent is to support tissue that may need more than a growth-factor boost to calm down and function better.

Neither one regrows a joint on command, reverses years of wear, or works identically for every patient. Regenerative approaches work gradually, over weeks, as your body responds — and the amount and speed of that response varies from person to person. Be skeptical of any source — online or in person — that promises a specific outcome before a physician has even examined your joint.

## So which one is actually right for you?

This is the question people are really asking when they search "PRP vs stem cells," and the honest answer is: it depends on your joint, not on which treatment sounds more advanced. A few patterns that tend to hold:

- **Milder, earlier-stage joint pain** — stiffness that comes and goes, early wear, tendon-related irritation — is often where PRP alone is a reasonable starting point.
- **More advanced wear**, a joint that hasn't improved with PRP or other conservative care, or a patient who has already explored simpler options, is where a physician is more likely to discuss adding donor-derived stem cells.
- **Some patients** end up with a combined approach rather than an either/or choice, since PRP and stem cells support the repair process in different ways.

None of this is a substitute for an actual evaluation. Imaging, history, and a physical exam of the joint tell a physician far more than a general comparison article ever can — including this one. ## Who tends to be a candidate for each

Good candidates for **PRP** typically have early-to-moderate joint irritation, want a non-surgical option with no donor material involved, and are looking for a lower-intensity first step. Good candidates for **stem cell joint regeneration** typically have more significant joint wear, haven't gotten enough relief from PRP or other conservative measures, or are specifically trying to delay or avoid joint replacement surgery.

Not everyone is a candidate for either one. Certain joint conditions, active infections, some autoimmune diagnoses, or structural damage that's too advanced for regenerative support may point toward a different plan entirely — including, in some cases, a referral for surgical evaluation. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is PRP the same as stem cell therapy?

No. PRP is made from your own blood and contains no donor material. Stem cell therapy at Utah Stem Cells uses stem cells from donated umbilical cord and amniotic tissue, not cells taken from your own body. They're different materials, sourced differently, used for different situations.

### Is stem cell therapy FDA-approved?

Donor-derived stem cell procedures for joint pain are not FDA-approved as a cure for arthritis or joint disease. They are offered as a regenerative option whose goal is to support the body's own repair processes, with results that vary from person to person.

### Which one works faster?

Neither produces instant results. Both PRP and stem cell therapy work gradually as the body responds over the following weeks, and timelines vary by patient, joint, and the extent of the underlying wear.

### Do I need an MRI before deciding?

Not always, but imaging can help the physician assess how advanced the joint wear is, which directly affects whether PRP alone, stem cell joint regeneration, or another approach entirely is the more reasonable recommendation.
