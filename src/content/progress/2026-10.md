completed:
  - title: "Patient privacy review of the oldest articles"
    detail: "Three old articles that showed individual patients were taken offline: one with a patient's full name and health details, one with a named patient's treatment diary, and one with six photos of a patient's face. None had been seen in search in sixteen months, so nothing was lost. In a fourth, the patient's name was removed and the page kept. The four patient quotes in the most-read article, which did not come from the clinic's patients, were rewritten as clearly labelled illustrative scenarios, with the page address and structure unchanged. One thing to settle on the clinic's side: before any patient story is published again, a signed written authorization should be on file."
  - title: "Ten older articles corrected so they match how the clinic actually works"
    detail: "The articles carried over from the previous website, written between 2017 and 2020, did not all agree with the clinic's current practice. Several said stem cells are taken from the patient's own body, with claims such as no risk of rejection, when the clinic uses donated umbilical cord and amniotic cells. Others said things like proven or painless, which no one can promise. Four were rewritten, one that reproduced a published study under the clinic's name now summarizes it with full credit to its authors, and the rest were corrected sentence by sentence. This matters beyond tidiness: AI assistants compare sources, and a website that contradicts itself is a weaker one to quote."
  - title: "Blog articles made easier for search engines and AI assistants to read"
    detail: "Search is moving from lists of links to assistants such as ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity that answer in one go and name their sources. To be quoted, a page has to be easy to read by a machine. Each article's questions and answers are now also published in the structured form those systems read directly (111 questions across 19 articles), together with who wrote it, his role as Medical Director, and when it was last modified. This removes obstacles; it does not guarantee a citation, and no one can promise one."
  - title: "Prices now published on the stem cell page"
    detail: "With the clinic's go-ahead, the stem cell joint regeneration page and the IV stem cell therapy page now state their pricing: $6,000 for the first treatment and $2,200 for every treatment after that. On the joint page that holds for the same area or a different one. It is also written as a question and answer, because \"stem cell therapy cost\" is already searched for around the clinic, with the site showing up and almost nobody clicking, since the page gave no answer. The automatic articles may now quote the same two figures when it is relevant. No other treatment shows a price."
  - title: "Three articles published, all on stem cells and PRP"
    detail: "\"Umbilical Cord Stem Cells: Why Donated Tissue and Not Your Own\" on 1 October, \"Stem Cell Injections for Back Pain: What the Evidence Shows\" on 8 October and \"Stem Cell Hair Restoration: Does It Actually Work\" on 15 October. Researched, written, illustrated and published without a manual step."
  - title: "The editorial plan now follows the search data"
    detail: "We went through sixteen months of Google Search Console and rebuilt the schedule around stem cells and PRP — the treatments that paid advertising is not allowed to promote, so search has to carry them. Every topic was picked where the site is already close to the top of the results. Twenty-five topics are queued, roughly six months."
  - title: "Galleri email sequence written and designed"
    detail: "Six emails for the Galleri test, each with its own branded image and each closing with the test maker's own safety information. They follow the same careful line as the Galleri page: what the test is, what it is not, and that it adds to routine screening rather than replacing it."
  - title: "Three Galleri emails sent, and the promotional pricing added"
    detail: "The first went out on 9 October, the second on 14 October and the third on 26 October, each reaching about 3,400 people. They were opened by 19.9%, 14.8% and 15.2%. Clicks stayed low throughout: six, six and five. The third carried the clinic's promotional pricing for the first time — $1,000 for the test alongside IV stem cell therapy, $1,200 on its own, against $1,400 normally — and it did not lift the click rate, though the people who did click went to the Galleri page rather than the home page. One thing to settle on the clinic's side: the promotion was described as ending on 31 October, but the emails carry no end date, so the remaining three in November and December will still quote those prices."

  - title: "A twelve-email series written on what stem cell treatment costs"
    detail: "A second campaign, separate from Galleri and built entirely around the pricing now published on the site: $6,000 for the first treatment and $2,200 for every one after, for any area of the body. Twelve emails, each with its own branded image, covering the offer itself, what happens when a second joint needs treatment, where the cells come from, and the point that price has nothing to do with whether someone is a candidate — that stays a physician's decision. Three of the twelve use no photography at all, because the argument is clearer as a diagram than as a stock image."

  - title: "The stem cell emails are outperforming the Galleri ones four to one"
    detail: "Sent to the same 3,400 people, in the same week, from the same address. The Galleri emails drew 0.15% to 0.17% clicks; the first stem cell email drew 0.65%, and every one of those clicks went to the treatment page rather than the home page. Unsubscribes were lower too: 14 against 26. The difference is not the design or the sending — it is that a published price answers a question people already have, while a screening test asks them to think about something they were not thinking about."

  - title: "A smaller, warmer audience tested alongside the main list"
    detail: "The mailing list holds 4,533 contacts, but 967 have already unsubscribed, leaving about 3,566 reachable. Within those, 120 people are former or prospective joint-treatment patients. They receive all twelve stem cell emails, twice a week; the main list receives five of them, spaced out. The first email to the smaller group drew 2.33% clicks against 0.65% from the main list, though the two that followed drew none, so this is a signal worth watching rather than a conclusion."

  - title: "Sixteen more sends scheduled through mid-December"
    detail: "The two campaigns alternate rather than overlap, so nobody receives both in the same week, and the 120 people in the smaller group are excluded from the main-list sends to avoid writing to them twice. Everything is scheduled to arrive at 10:00 Utah time and sits within the monthly sending allowance, with room to spare."
  - title: "Calls to action rewritten after the first results"
    detail: "Plenty of people opened the first email but few clicked. Someone reading about a screening test wants to understand it before thinking about booking, so the buttons now invite them to keep reading, with booking as a quieter line underneath. The second email already carried the new wording and drew about the same number of clicks, so we are watching the next sends before drawing conclusions."
  - title: "New page for the Galleri multi-cancer early detection test"
    detail: "A blood test that looks for a signal shared by more than 50 cancers now has its own page, under a new Early Detection & Screening section in the menu. It is written cautiously on purpose: the page states up front that the test is not FDA-approved, that it adds to routine screening rather than replacing it, that it does not detect every cancer, and that a positive result is not a diagnosis. That honesty is what makes a page like this defensible — and it is what patients deciding whether to test actually need."
  - title: "Location pages pointed at what people actually search for"

People search \"stem cell therapy\" plus their town — about 4,700 searches over the past sixteen months, and the pages were sitting on page three or four for all of them. Retitled, with the web addresses left untouched so nothing breaks."
